The training

Not a collection of isolated injuries

Most lower limb treatment focuses on the hip flexors, the knee ligaments, or the plantar fascia. The assumption is that the problem is local. The treatment reflects that assumption.

This course starts elsewhere. The lower limb does not operate in isolation. It is part of a continuous fascial network anchored at the pelvis, the body's central hub, where the hip, knee, ankle, and foot serve as way stations for load transfer and fluid movement, and the ligaments and tendons function as afferent highways to the nervous system.

If you treat the patellar tendon without addressing its link to the quadriceps and the iliotibial band, you overload the tibial tuberosity. If you mobilize the ankle without addressing fascial chain continuity back to the pelvis, you are moving structure without restoring function.

This course teaches you to treat the lower limb as an integrated fascial system, not a collection of isolated injuries.

The goal of movement and therapy is this: create the highest level of proprioceptive input to the brain so the body can move efficiently, powerfully, and adapt faster than the demands placed on it. Neurological rehabilitation is not limited to balance drills, visual training, or juggling exercises. It depends on the quality of the receptors in the fascia: the ligaments, tendons, periosteum, and capsules. If those structures are not mechanically sound and sensorially responsive, no amount of movement training will correct the underlying deficit.

Those with the best fascia win. This course gives you the assessment framework and manual skills to address the root cause of lower limb dysfunction, starting at the pelvis. Not the symptom, the structure.

What you'll learn

  • Fascial chain treatment The fascial chains of the lower limb include the hip, knee, ankle, and foot as the primary regulators of load transfer and fluid movement. Normalization is not soft tissue work. It is osteoarticular pumping combined with chain-specific fascial integration, traced back to the pelvis at every step.
  • How to identify the true fascial chains of the lower limb (not muscle chains: load and fluid pathways)
  • The role of the hip joint capsule as the primary link between the pelvis and lower limb mechanics
  • Osteoarticular pumping techniques for the hip, knee, ankle, and foot
  • How fascial chain dysfunction in the lower limb creates compensatory tension patterns that appear at the pelvis and spine
  • 2TLS (transverse tendon and ligament stretching) 2TLS is not a general stretching protocol. It is a specific manual technique designed to improve the individuality of tendon and ligament structures while upgrading the quality of afferent input from those tissues.
  • How to free the fibers of tendon from each other
  • How to free the fibers of ligament from each other
  • How to alter the thixotropy (fluid viscosity) of water within the fibers and extracellular matrix
  • How to use 2TLS on the Achilles tendon, patellar tendon, and collateral ligaments to influence afferent input and release protective tension patterns
  • If the fascial link between the plantar fascia and the Achilles tendon is not free and flexible, the posterior chain compensates all the way to the pelvis. In the biotensegrity model, these links are structural.
