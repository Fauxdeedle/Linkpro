Content Ideas:
    1.    Most footwear young athletes use and potential negative side effects
    1.    Stiff and narrow toe box - restrict movement and weaken muscles of the foot - especially those that contribute to the natural arch of the foot and toes
    2.    Thick soles reduce sensory feedback from foot - this information is critical for the brains ability to process information about the ground and environment, leading to muscular mbalances, poor posture and issues with balance/stability
    3.    We only train barefoot and put emphasis on training specific proprioception as well as strengthening the intrinsic muscles of the foot to create a strong healthy foot that doesn’t rely on footwear for support.
    2.    Less sport specific and more basic coordination and movement literacy focus
    1.    Too much sport specific training at a young age has many cons. Much more likely to create muscular imbalances, postural deviations, suffer from overuse injuries as well as the risk of mental fatigue and burnout
    2.    It is important for young athletes to be exposed to many different kinds of movements, not just those specific their sport. During early developmental stages it is important to be exposed to many different movements to develop “physical literacy”, the ability to move competently and confidently through a variety of physical activities. “The ability to reduce, stabilize and produce force required in basic movements” - Bill Knowles. This creates an overall better athlete, regardless of the sport they chose to pursue. 
    3.    There must be a focus on addressing weak points and imbalances which is individual to each young athlete. Many programs follow a one size fits all ideology, which will not benefit every athlete equally.
    3.    Young athletes have a big tendency to overtrain
    1.    Overtraining is a big problem for any athlete, but at the youth level it seems to be more prevalent.
    2.    Cannot stay in the same phase of training year round. A lof of athletes stay in a higher intensity strength phase for too long
    3.    High intensity training is very taxing on the nervous system. When the CNS does not have time to recover, force output and athletic performance decreases. It also leads to mental burnout and fatigue. All of these factors not only decrease performance, but also pose a much greater risk for injury.
    4.    Knowing the anatomy
    1.    The anatomy is much more complex than a lot of training programs respect. For example the hamstring is a very commonly injured muscle in many sports. However we have 3 hamstrings, and most programs do not respect this. There not only is a specific exercise for each hamstring muscle, but also the different parts of said muscle. You can choose an exercise that focuses more on the hamstring attachment at the knee, or more of that up by the pelvis. Depending on the individual athlete, especially if they already have a history of hamstring injury, it is very important to prescribe the proper exercise.
    2.    Also, it is the WEAK point that pays the price. Weak low abs, bad biomechanics of pelvis can lead to chronic inflammation/overuse of the hamstring which then pays the price. Need to train more than JUST the hamstring
    5.     Imitating play and keeping things fun
    1.    It is also important to add an element of play and fun to the training. Creating games within the training and exercise can help keep young athletes more engaged. 
    6.    Basics and Foundations
    1.    Jumping, Running, Pushing, Pulling, Lunging, Squatting, Twisting

MISSING Pieces in throwing and pitching.  10/29/25

    ◦    SEGMENTAL PROFICIENCY.  Specifically the thoracic spine and rib cage.  Gaming, bad posture (rounding of upper back) and forward head posture are the silent killers of your kids athleticism and specifically throwing…. In brief:
    ◦    Lacking the ability of separation in the upper cervical (neck) and upper back (thoracic), hinders an athletes ability fixate on an object (catcher’s mitt or hitting a moving ball) while moving the head and trunk.  There are no excuses why this is missing from your kids ‘athletic training regiment’.  If you are serious about both performance and prevention, this is non negotiable.
    ◦    The entire body is involved in properly throwing.   Literally from the back foot big toe to the distal joint of fingers.  This is a symphony in motion!  Some pieces are for precision and others for power.  Does your training respect this?  Where are you leaking energy in the overall “whip” that’s occurring through your body?
    ◦    In our experience, most young athletes are all missing the same pieces of the puzzle…
    ◦    1. deceleration of entire scapula girdle and trunk after release
    ◦    2. Transfer of energy from lower body through pelvis and to the trunk. This requires finesse not more force,  especially in young athletes.
    ◦    3. Weak feet and ankles…. This guarantees less push off and ineffective deceleration thereafter.
